Research

Two projects from UCLA's year-long Data, Justice, and Society cluster, September 2025 to June 2026. Both ask who a model leaves out.

Space, Time, and Spatial Injustice

A spatial regression of pedestrian footfall in New York City

Planners often model foot traffic with ordinary least squares, which treats every street as if its neighbors did not exist. I tested that assumption on the New York City Department of Transportation's Bi-Annual Pedestrian Counts, using three synthesized stand-in predictors for distance to transit, retail density and civic space.

The practical point is that a model which ignores geography reads a quiet street as a street nobody wants, and steers investment toward hubs that are already busy. I also wrote up the limits: the spatial term can overfit to local noise, and straight-line distance ignores barriers such as freeways.

Built in Python with pandas, GeoPandas, libpysal and spreg, in a Google Colab notebook.

Driving in the Dark

An essay on where autonomous vehicles get tested, and where they don't. March 2026

Autonomous vehicle makers test where mapping is easy and customers are dense, such as the wide grids of Phoenix and the corridors of San Francisco. I call the places left out data deserts: regions where a lack of high-definition sensor data and local testing makes the systems unreliable.

I read California DMV disengagement reports from 2020 to 2025 and found root causes logged in wording too vague to audit. The essay proposes a public-interest data repository, overseen by an equity board housed within a federal body like the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, so regulators can see where these systems fail.

I presented the findings to the Active Mobility department at Singapore's Land Transport Authority.
